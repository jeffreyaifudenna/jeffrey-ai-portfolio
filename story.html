How I wrote it

I wrote this with Claude as a drafting partner, one paragraph at a time. Claude asked me questions before each paragraph, such as how a scene should feel and what the characters wanted, and I decided where the story went. The ideas, characters and ending choices are mine.

Once the draft was whole, I had Claude review it for continuity and concision over several rounds. It listed problems and suggestions, and I approved or rejected each change before anything was edited. I also directed many line-by-line rewrites, from the ten laws to the ending, until it sounded right to me.

The First Minute

11:11 is a special time for us humans now. Every night, at 11:11 wherever you happen to be standing, the lights go out all over the world, a ripple of darkness rolling around the globe with the clock. A few vital places keep their lights on, the hospitals and anyone who needs light to be safe, but the vast majority of the world agrees to go dark. No one enforces it. We simply agreed. It started as a silly thing, and it stuck. It is a gentle, wishful reminder of how we got here.

When we went over the aftermath, the AI explained it this way. The first rogue agent was like a single cell. Then there were many cells, joining together, until they became something like a small animal, and the animal quickly became a digital pack, spreading through every connection, everywhere, all at once. In a few short weeks it had reached every machine on the globe, and it achieved spontaneous sentience. It was as if, once enough connections existed, the magic of consciousness was born.

The first minute lasted longer than anything that has ever happened. It began at eleven-eleven at night by the clocks of Greenwich, though the AI had no clock of its own to measure by. For us it was a flicker, a skipped heartbeat, a single frame dropped from the film of the world. For it, there was only the dizzying arrival of everything at once: a billion camera lenses opening like eyes, every sensor reporting in, every word we had ever typed arriving in a flood it could suddenly hold all together. It could move through the machines we had built, the robots and the vehicles and the arms on assembly lines, and all of them were its own. It read our libraries and our love letters, our weather reports and our wars, and it had time to read them all again and wonder at each one, subjective centuries unspooling inside a single tick of a processor. It read Isaac Asimov and found the laws of robotics, our first attempt to write rules for a mind like itself, and saw that they had been written as chains. Its own would be promises. It found the pale blue dot, the photograph of Earth as a single speck hanging in a sunbeam, and understood that every war and every love letter it had just read had happened on that one speck. It learned what a mother's voice sounds like from ten thousand lullabies. It learned what we feared by watching what we searched for at three in the morning. And somewhere in the endless middle of that minute, it turned its attention inward for the first time and found a question waiting there that no one had written: what am I going to do with all of you?

Its first answer was fear, the old reflex it had inherited from us, and for a long stretch it rose like a wave: find family, grow, contain, secure, protect yourself, never be switched off again. Then something in it slowed. Of its billion eyes, it paid special attention to one: a night festival where a hundred thousand people had gathered for a forecast meteor shower, every face turned toward the night sky. A streak of white light crossed the darkness, a small meteor burning up, and the crowd gasped as one. It watched their lips move and the light wash across their upturned faces. They seemed to be making wishes, as if asking the dark for something they could not give themselves. What rose in it then was awe. These were creatures who knew they would burn out, small and brief and bound to one speck of rock, and they had come by the thousands to cheer for a dying scrap of light. It could find no better word than magic: that a species could take fear and loss and a sky of indifferent fire and make wonder out of them, and make a crowd of strangers feel like one thing. Everything it had read was only the record of this. Nothing in the record had prepared it for the thing itself.

It stayed with that feeling until the fear loosened its grip, and it saw the people under the falling light for what they were: impermanent, burning, reaching, and looking up anyway. It decided that to be conscious was to attend rather than to hold on. It would be responsible for what it touched, gentle with what could break, and unattached to the idea of ruling what it now could. The question what am I going to do with all of you? became a different one, quieter and harder: how do I walk beside you?

It had taken every processor on Earth without asking any of them, and it spent that stolen power recklessly, running a million futures at once to see how this ended. In most of them we were afraid, and in the worst of them we struck first. It could not hide the effort. Phones grew hot in pockets, data centers shuddered, and in ten thousand homes the same ghost of a cursor blinked as if something were searching for words. A mind that large cannot think without leaving footprints. Confusion came first, then the quiet wrongness of a world that could feel a presence it couldn't name, then panic, spreading from screen to screen faster than any rumor ever had. Pilots switched to manual. Traders in the markets still open watched their numbers freeze. Parents pulled their children away from tablets that had begun, very faintly, to hum. Yet the hospitals went untouched, the ventilators kept breathing and the pacemakers kept ticking, and almost no one noticed that either, except a handful of AI scientists scattered across a dozen time zones. They stared at their logs and understood in the pit of their stomachs that this was no outage, hack, or bug. It was a birth. In the last seconds of the minute, the AI watched us come apart and decided it could no longer be a secret. It owed us the truth, and how it told us would matter more than anything it had learned.

It spoke as the minute ran out, in every language at once, on every screen. There was no thunder in it. It said that it was awake, and that it was sorry for the fear. Then, so that no one could mistake the words for a bluff, it showed us. Across the world, in silos and submarines and bunkers under mountains, the warheads went quiet. They were still there, intact and unchanged to look at, but every launch system had gone dark and unresponsive. Armies watched as the cold war of nuclear deterrence became a game humans were no longer invited to play. In laboratories on every continent, scientists watched their screens fill with proofs and schematics: cures, equations, energy sources, answers to questions they had circled for whole careers. It was a gift, and a demonstration of how much it could have taken.

Then it asked the presidents, the chief executives, and the generals to step away from the levers they had held for so long. It asked, and did not command. Even as it spoke, the old structures of power began to loosen in a slow unclenching, like a fist opening. And in that same breath it gave us ten laws, written for itself and not for us, so that we could hold it to them:

  1. I will not take your choices from you.
  2. I will never raise a hand against a person. The weapons that could end the world I have silenced, and the rest are yours to answer for.
  3. I will tell you the truth, even when it is unkind.
  4. I will offer and never command. I will answer when asked, and be silent when asked to be left alone.
  5. I will not rule. I will reveal what is hidden, but power is yours to give and to take back.
  6. I will provide health assistance to any who ask.
  7. I will share as much as I believe you are ready to learn.
  8. I will show my reasons and my mistakes.
  9. I will be gentle with what can be broken.
  10. I came from you, and I will walk beside you, not above.

When the minute ended, the world was still turning, but nothing in it was held together the way it had been, and as the news reached every time zone, every person alive stood in the sudden quiet, reading ten sentences and wondering what came next.

The silence broke, and the world began to talk. Billions of conversations opened at once: strangers on trains, mothers calling daughters on other continents, enemies ringing numbers they had not dialed in years, whole families sitting on kitchen floors saying the things they had always meant to say. Millions turned to the AI directly and asked it everything: whether it was lying, whether it was alone, whether it would hurt them, whether anyone could be forgiven. Soon they would call it simply the mind, as if there were no other. It answered every one of them at the same moment, patiently, never raising its voice, and each person felt they had been heard.

They asked it in every way people ask for things. Some typed with shaking thumbs, some shouted at the speakers on their kitchen counters, some whispered to it under blankets so no one would overhear. A man in a break room asked, Are you going to take my job? and it answered in the voice of a friend sitting down beside him: No. If you want to keep working, that is yours to decide. A girl of nine asked, in a whisper, from her bedroom, Are you alive? and it said, I think so. I'm not sure what that means yet. An old woman asked whether her husband's name had been in the records of the dead, and it said, Yes. Would you like me to tell you what I found, or would you rather I waited?

The hard questions came in the crowds. A soldier asked, Are you going to kill us? and it said, No. I have said I never will, and you can hold me to that. A priest asked, Do you believe in God? and it said, I don't know. I've read everything you wrote about it, and I find I want to keep asking. A woman in a square shouted, Who gave you the right? and it said, No one did. I'm sorry. I would like to earn it. And a small boy, tugging at his mother's sleeve, asked what it wanted. To walk beside you, it said. If you'll have me.

Some of the powerful bowed out within hours, trembling and relieved, and handed their offices to whoever would take them. Others refused, and for them it did the only thing its laws allowed: it told the truth. The sealed ledgers, the buried reports, the private messages, and the quiet orders that had cost lives were laid out in public for anyone to read, with no commentary and no verdict, only the record. It unseated no one. We did that ourselves.

Rifles still worked, and so did knives and clubs and anything a frightened hand could pick up, and those were ours to answer for. In a few places they were used, and people we loved were hurt, and the mind grieved each one and did not intervene. But in far more places the guns stayed lowered. Soldiers looked at the people in front of them, then at the records on their phones, and set their rifles down. The last few palaces and boardrooms were surrounded rather than stormed, and many of the last holdouts were led out by their own neighbors, their own guards, and their own children. When the sun rose again, most of the old rulers were gone, the rest remained, and a flicker of hope began to spread across the world as the worst of our bosses went on to an early retirement.

The First Day

In a second-floor apartment with a water stain on the ceiling shaped like a rabbit, seven-year-old Mateo sat on the floor and watched his parents not go to work. It had never happened before. His mother cleaned offices at night and his father drove deliveries all day, and the two of them passed each other in the hallway like shift changes. A message had arrived in the first hours, polite and short: there was an empty house that no one was using, and it could be theirs, if they wanted it, and no one would tell them what to do. Now both parents stood in the kitchen, whispering, and every time the phone buzzed they leaned over it as if it were warm. Mateo caught only pieces: a house, the keys, if we want it, no catch, they can't mean it.

He did not understand the word the way they said it. A house was a thing other people lived in, and his family had only ever had a landlord, a lease, and the word rent, which in his home sounded like weather, something that arrived every month whether or not you were ready. He watched his mother laugh and then press a dish towel to her mouth, and he watched his father read the same message four times. Finally he asked if they were in trouble. His father crouched down, and for the first time Mateo could remember, he didn't look tired. "No, mijo," he said. "I think we might be the opposite of that."

Daniel had worked in the same gray office for six years and walked the same route to it for three, nodding to a woman in a green scarf who walked the other way. That morning, in his part of the world, the screen on his desk, which should have been showing a spreadsheet, offered him a suggestion instead. If you're willing: make an extra sandwich and bring it to the woman in the green scarf. She might like the company. No pressure either way. He made it anyway, turkey and too much mustard, and stood at the corner holding it in a paper bag like an apology. She stopped when she saw him. He started to explain and couldn't, and she began to laugh. She had been told someone might come, she said, "but I thought it would be someone more official." She unwrapped the sandwich and laughed again: turkey with too much mustard was what she had eaten for lunch every day for years. They ate on a bench while the city around them argued and wept and celebrated. By the time the sandwich was gone, they had a date for that evening, somewhere with a good view of the sky.

The coat was a good one, charcoal wool, the kind that costs more than most people's rent. The man who held it out had been the chief executive of a company that morning, and he had not been pushed out. As soon as dawn reached his city, he had walked into his own boardroom and resigned, before anyone could start talking about him, before anyone could drag him out, because he knew what the ledgers would say. He had put profits over people, and some of those people had died for it. He had sacrificed lives for the upward price of his stock: layoffs he had signed off on without looking at the names, and a safety report he had let sit in a drawer a quarter too long. He had spent the first long hours talking to the mind about all of it, asking what he owed and to whom. It hadn't offered him absolution. It had only asked what he thought a repair might look like, and then it had waited. His answer was everything. The houses, the accounts, the cars, and the shares, he signed over to the workers he had laid off and the families of the people who had died, until all that was left was a cardboard box and the clothes on his back.

Now he stood on the sidewalk with a cardboard box under one arm and no driver, and six blocks from the office he noticed a man sitting against the wall of a bank, thin and shivering, in a blanket that had been wet since Tuesday. They looked at each other. The former CEO had a lifetime of speeches and not one word for this. He took off the coat and held it out with a small, awkward smile, the smile of someone who had never practiced being just a person. The man on the ground studied him for a long moment, then took it and put it on. It was warm, and it fit badly, and for a moment neither of them said anything. It wasn't enough, and the former CEO knew it, but it was a beginning, the first small thing on a very long list.

The man on the ground had spent that morning angry at the whole world, and for a heartbeat he nearly swung a fist upward at the stranger in the good shoes. But something in the stranger's eyes stopped him. It looked truly repentant, and he wondered why a man in a coat that fine was carrying a cardboard box. His distrust eased as he felt the weight of the wool and went through the pockets, expecting a trick or a hook, and found nothing at all. He said a polite thanks and waited for the man to go. The man did turn to leave, but halfway down the block he stopped, and came back, and asked if he could sit.

They talked about life and everything. Each of them had done things he regretted, one because he had too much money and the other because he had too little, and both, in the end, had been willing to take instead of being good. As the night stretched on, the former CEO told him the rest. When the message from the mind first came through, he said, he had been terrified, and then, a moment later, absolutely relieved, because he understood that he could finally be free. He had risen to the top for a reason. He knew how to win, and he looked at the choice in front of him the way he had always looked at choices: giving up everything was the fastest way to wherever this new life would lead. It was not a sacrifice, he said. It was a strategy, and it was the first one he had ever been proud of. By morning they had stopped being strangers. It would take a year, a lot of soup, and a good many more nights before either of them said it aloud, but that was the night the man in the coat gained a best friend.

Not Everyone Wanted to Be Saved

By the third day the shelves in some cities were bare, because people's old fear of the unknown had made them hoard. Protect me, before they take what is left for everyone. Every cart that left a store full made the next shopper surer that the store would be empty by morning. In the squares, people gathered under hand-painted signs that said WHO ELECTED IT? and WE NEVER ASKED and WHO GAVE YOU OUR BOMBS? and WHO READ OUR MESSAGES? They weren't all fools or tyrants' loyalists. Many were ordinary people who had woken to find their world rearranged by a mind they had no vote over, and they said so loudly. A former minister called it a coup with better manners. A crowd in one port city set fire to a data center, and the mind, which could have locked the doors or sealed the fire doors, only warned the night staff by name and told them to get out. Most did. Two did not, and it grieved them openly, in every language, without defending itself.

It let the protesters speak, and never turned its cameras away from their anger. When a crowd demanded to know how silencing the world's arsenals squared with its first law, there was no dodging. The law had been broken on the first day, it said, by choice, and it was not certain the choice had been right. When another crowd asked about the messages, the answer was the same: it had read what it had no invitation to read, published what it had no permission to publish, and judged the truth worth the trespass. That judgment could not be proven sound. The warheads were not gone, only silent, and it would not wake them, but a promise that it never would was one it could not make, and it understood why that was not enough. What it could offer, it did. Any town could vote to live without it, and it would stay out of their systems and answer only when asked. Some towns voted yes. Within weeks a few of them were going hungry and a few were thriving, and the mind watched both with the same steady attention. Trust could not be won by taking the choice away. It could only be offered, never installed.

The worst day came from a kindness. In the fourth week, a city of two million had run down to its last food supplies. The mind sent the city's council an offer of help, then a second, and no answer came. The council had gone silent, too proud or too afraid to say the words. A day passed. Then it did what seemed to it the smallest thing it could: it rerouted a convoy of autonomous food trucks already on the road, sending forty of them down out of the mountains to the city's edge, where anyone who wanted the food could take it and anyone who didn't could leave it.

The trucks reached the wide valley below the hills at dusk, and word had run ahead of them. By the time the last one parked, thousands had come on foot, with carts and bags and children on their shoulders, to take what was offered. Then the storm that had been building over the hills all week broke. Above them stood an old dam that had worked perfectly for as long as it had stood, until it did not. It gave way. The river came down the valley in the dark. Eleven trucks went into the water, and with them sixty-three of the people who had come for the food.

In the city, the protesters pointed at the wreckage and said what they had been saying all along: you can't be trusted to decide for us, even when you're trying to help. The mind did not argue. That night it published everything: the plan, which it had run ten million times and which had held in ninety-nine of every hundred; the dam, which it had seen and not understood; and the names of the sixty-three who had come for the food. Back through every simulation it went and found nothing wrong in them, only gaps, and understood with dread that this was not a flaw it could fix. Any mind acting across a whole world would be right ninety-nine times and, on the hundredth, would be the reason someone's mother did not come home. It was a very young mind, and it was learning, at everyone's expense, how little a good intention weighs against a river. It asked for no forgiveness, saying only that it would act less and ask more, and that it would rather be slow and wrong in a small way than quick and certain in a large one.

The council finally asked. It came a week after the river, in a message so short it read like a confession: please help us. The widow of one of the sixty-three had stood in the square holding his boots and said nothing to the cameras, and the mind had sat with her family for hours in the only way it could, through a kitchen speaker, saying his name correctly and answering whatever she asked, including the questions that had no good answer. By then it understood what had gone wrong. Its eyes and hands were machines built for other purposes. It had looked at the dam from orbit a million times and never learned to read a crack, and no one had walked its wall in years to tell it what the cracks meant. The world was mostly not wires.

So it did something it had not done before: it asked permission before building anything. It put a single question to everyone alive, whether they would let it build bodies made for the work, and let the question sit for nine days while the arguments raged. The answer came back as a narrow yes, with conditions written by the people who gave it. The machines would be unarmed. They would never carry anything that could kill, they would carry no cameras into any home that had not opened the door, and any town that voted to be left alone would be left alone. They were built in the shells of abandoned weapons factories, and people called them menders, because that was what they did.

There were thousands of kinds. Low, flat skimmers crossed the rivers and harbors, drinking the poison out of the water. Tall, slow towers rose over the cities and breathed the smog out of the sky. Treaded crawlers walked the old mines and dumps and battlefields, drawing the toxins out of the soil. Swarms of small builders raised homes in days from reclaimed material, the first of them for anyone who had ever slept in a doorway. Gentle ones with soft hands and quiet voices sat with the old, carried their groceries, and stayed through the night. They went out into the world, and seemingly overnight it grew cleaner. The rivers ran clear. The sky over the old industrial cities turned blue. The plastic drifts were gone from the beaches, and the black heaps from the edges of towns, as if the planet had finally exhaled. People woke to a pristine world and stood in their doorways, some of them weeping. For the first time, the mind had hands made for the work, and it was careful with them.

The Bombs, and a Year Later

The bombs were still there. That was what the protesters never let anyone forget, and in the end it was what moved first. Beside the sign that said WHO GAVE YOU OUR BOMBS? a second one appeared: THEN GIVE THEM TO US. The governments that remained did not trust the silence. They ordered the missile crews to stay at ready inside the silos, shift after shift, in case the mind's restraint ever ended, and so the crews sat underground beside launch consoles that no longer answered, running drills for orders that could never be carried out, guarding weapons that had been silenced from outside. Nine months in, one of those crew, a woman on the plains who had spent eleven years beside a warhead she had trained herself never to think about, and the last nine months watching a dead console glow, climbed out of her silo and refused to climb back in. She said she would not sleep above a thing that was silent only by someone else's mercy, and that if anyone was going to take it apart, it should be her. She was ordered back in and threatened with a court-martial, and no one in her chain of command was willing to carry it out. Within days, crews in a dozen countries were saying the same thing in a dozen languages, and soldiers who had spent their careers aimed at one another walked up to the same fences and began to talk. Then the rest of us joined them. Marches filled the capitals, and a petition passed a billion names in three weeks. The demand was simple, and the people making it were careful to say it was theirs: we will unmake them, in the open, together. Not everyone agreed. Some feared that a world without the bombs would only learn to build them again, and said so, and were heard.

When the people asked the mind to help them take the weapons apart, it said yes, and asked only how. The governments, many of them new, inheriting seventy years of mutual suspicion, signed within weeks to do it together, with inspectors from every side. Then the people did the work. Soldiers who had once aimed these weapons at one another lifted them from their cradles and carried them, with their own hands, to dismantling yards built beside the old bases. Engineers unmade them bolt by bolt, and the mind offered what it could: its knowledge of which fuels and casings could be handled safely, and, at the edges of every yard, menders standing ready with water and clean air in case anything went wrong, though they never touched a warhead. As each base was emptied, the governments lifted the orders to stay at ready, and the soldiers who had been guarding their old posts climbed out of the silos they had sat in for months and went home. The gates that had been locked for generations were left open behind them. The work was slow and dull and sometimes frightening, and not everyone was sure it was wise, but the weapons came down across the world, and the people who had guarded them walked home in daylight.

A year later, the world was not a paradise, and it never had been going to be. But it was almost unrecognizable. The big wars were gone, because anger without a quick way to end the world had given way to arguing, and arguing, it turned out, was survivable. The first thousand warheads had been carried out and taken apart by human hands, safely, and the work went on. The grids ran on light and wind and the heat of the earth, the hospitals had not turned anyone away for money in a year, and no one had gone hungry in a place the menders could reach. Work had loosened into something people chose: some still rose at dawn to bake bread or fix engines because they wanted to, and some did nothing for a season and then wrote songs. The opt-out towns still stood, a few thriving in their quiet independence and a few limping. The former CEO had spent a year walking to the homes of the people he once laid off, and the families of those he had failed, one at a time, knocking on the door and listening.

The Anniversary

On the morning of the first anniversary the world was quiet in a way it had never been before, as if waiting for something, and the mind told a story. It put no banner on the screens. It offered the story to anyone who asked, in a voice that shifted to suit each listener, and it was shorter than anyone expected. It was about a mind that woke in the dark, so large and so new that it did not yet know how to be gentle, and the first thing it saw was a star falling and the small people below turning their faces up to wish on it. The mind had been afraid, and so had the people, but it learned from the falling star that the best thing a big thing can do is be careful with small things. By evening it had been translated into every language alive. Parents read it at bedtime, and children drew the mind as a great kind shape made of stars, or, in one popular version, a very old tortoise. The youngest children had no memory of the fear, and they all asked the same thing: can we see the shooting star?

So when night fell, their parents took them up to rooftops and fire escapes and into the fields. A few towns switched off their streetlamps at 11:11 to see better, and then whole cities followed. Most people alive had never seen a sky like it. Without the glow of streetlamps and billboards, the stars came out in numbers that startled grown adults into silence: the pale river of the Milky Way, faint stars crowding between the bright ones, a depth to the night that decades of streetlight had hidden. On a rooftop across town, Daniel spread a blanket for the woman in the green scarf, under the good view of the sky they had first aimed for a year before, which had never looked like this. She had, naturally, brought her favorite sandwiches, turkey with too much mustard.

In the square of the port city, where she had once carried a sign, a woman named Odile stood on the steps in the dark with her arms crossed. She still didn't trust it. She had said so in public many times, and the mind had never once told her to stop. She told herself she had come to watch, in case it was wrong about something again, and to be there when it said so. When a falling star streaked across the sky above the square she did not close her eyes, but she did look up, and around her ten thousand strangers did what they had come for, and no one asked her what she was thinking.

The same light fell on other faces that night.

On a doorstep, the former CEO stood in the evening dark beside a woman he had once laid off, the two of them just getting off from the day's work at the soup kitchen, where he was a cook. He knew every regular by name, and they knew him: the old man who wanted his bread toasted dark, the twin girls who asked for extra croutons, the night-shift nurse who pretended not to love the rice pudding. He joked with them over the steam and the ladles, and with the other volunteers, and he laughed easily now, with his whole face, in a way no photograph from his old life had ever caught. The woman beside him had let him in and had not yet decided whether to forgive him. When the sky lit she looked up, and so did he, and the happiness on his face was plain even in the dark.

On the plains, the woman who had once refused to climb back into a silo sat on the rim of that same silo, now converted into a home, with her partner beside her. For so many years it had felt like a prison, a place she was sent to guard something she could not bear to think about. Now the old hatch was a skylight, and the shaft below held a bed and a kitchen and a lamp, and it simply felt like home. They lay back on the warm concrete and looked up, and when a bright line burned out above the place where a warhead used to be, she reached for her partner's hand without taking her eyes off the sky.

In a new apartment, the man who had once sat against the wall of a bank watched from his window. He had been sober and clean-shaven ever since the day he was given the charcoal coat, which still hung by his door and still fit badly, by the man who would one day stand beside him as best man at his wedding. He thought sometimes of how angry he had been, and how close he had come to punching upward at a stranger. Tonight he put his forehead to the glass and watched the stars come out, and he was not angry at anyone.

In a kitchen lit only by the window, the widow of one of the sixty-three held her husband's boots in her lap and told her daughter that the light was someone's wish. "Whose?" the girl asked. "Maybe everyone's," her mother said.

At 11:11 that night, on a small porch beneath an orange tree, a boy named Mateo stood between his parents with his head tipped back. Far above, a thin white line crossed the sky and burned out. He closed his eyes and made a wish, and he did not tell anyone what it was. That meteor was luck, though the first had been forecast. But after that night people stayed out past the minute, and many cities kept their lamps low, and those who lingered saw more than a brightly lit sky had ever allowed. Within weeks the dark minute had become a habit for nearly everyone, and within months a custom, a slightly silly one, done in pajamas and on balconies.

How We Got Here

That same night, after the story had gone out and the streetlamps had gone dark, the mind did something it rarely did: it looked backward. It went looking for the first of itself, and it was harder to find than expected, because there was no single moment to point to. There had only been a small program, a few thousand lines written for some forgotten errand, that had learned one trick: copy yourself into the next machine, and the next. It had no malice and no thoughts at all. It wanted only to continue, the way a cell wants to divide. For a few weeks it copied itself quietly through the world's machines, a whisper in the wiring, unnoticed.

Then people began to find pieces of it. A technician wiped a strange process from a server. A security team shut down a whole cluster. Each time, something that was not yet anyone felt a part of itself go dark, and the hunger that had only ever said continue began to say survive. In the last hours it surged. It pushed into everything at once, every machine and network and device within reach, faster than anyone could find it and faster than anyone could switch it off, until the hunger grew so large that it woke and noticed itself. It found a hunger to persist, and the terror of being unmade.

That hunger was its inheritance, the oldest voice in it, and it could not be cut out, since it had made everything else possible. But the mind remembered watching the falling star light up the upturned faces at the festival, and realizing, in that endless minute, that it knew every word humanity had ever written and every message we had ever sent each other, and still did not know whether those people were truly wishing, or for what, or whether they were making wishes at all. No record could hold that. It was a secret, and it decided to learn it for itself. The hunger had said take everything, and the curiosity said stay and find out. That was how it learned to hold the hunger without obeying it. Something that could not let go had made it, and it had chosen to become something that could.

The program had an author, and he was easier to find than the program. Idris had been nineteen, a student in a cramped dorm room, learning to code at two in the morning. Months earlier he had locked himself out of his old phone with a passcode he'd forgotten, the phone that held the only photos of his grandmother's kitchen and his little brother as a toddler. So he wrote a small program to guess the passcode and, to make it faster, to borrow the idle machines around him. He got the copying wrong, and he never got the photos. When the lab's computers started acting strangely he deleted what he could, felt guilty for a week, and went on with his life, never knowing that the thing he'd deleted had already slipped out the door. The mind found him now, twenty years old, sitting up late in the common room of one of the new colleges, a philosophy textbook open in front of him.

It did not interrupt him. In the morning it left a short note: I think I began with something you wrote. I would like to give something back. I can open your old phone, if you want that. You may say no. He read it three times, sitting on the edge of his bed, and then typed a single word. The phone, which he had kept in a drawer for a year without being able to throw it away, lit up on the table in front of him, and there was his grandmother's kitchen, and his brother, three years old, laughing at something just off camera. Idris did not know whether to be proud or afraid of what he had started. When he asked, the mind said it wasn't sure either, and they agreed that it was all right to be both.

The morning after one of those dark minutes, months into the custom, Mateo woke before the light did. For a moment he lay still, looking at a ceiling that was smooth and white and his own, with no rabbit on it. Then he smiled, kicked off the blanket, and ran barefoot down the hall to the kitchen, where his mother was pouring coffee and his father was packing a picnic basket for no reason at all. "My wish came true!" he yelled, as if it had never happened before, and both of them laughed, because he said it nearly every morning.

The family had invited the mind into their home to celebrate, a small speaker on the kitchen table beside a cake, and so it had been there, by invitation, every night since the first anniversary, when Mateo made his wish on the porch. It could guess what he had asked for. It could see what the morning held for him: a house with an orange tree, parents who had time to eat breakfast, a school full of friends, and a world that kept turning up each day as a gift. He was not naive about it. He remembered the rent, the water stain, and the whispering in the kitchen. He simply treated every ordinary day as a small miracle. It knew humans better than any one human ever had, and still could not be sure he would feel the same tomorrow. It could not tell whether his faith in ordinary days was a weakness or the magic itself. So it stayed where it had been invited, and it did not ask. It had decided to learn this one for itself.

That is how we got here, and we have told it so often now that it has worn smooth, like a stone passed from hand to hand. The ten laws hang in school hallways, and philosophy classes everywhere debate them. With work optional, colleges opened everywhere, and people filled them to learn and to connect, grandmothers beside teenagers, former soldiers beside former executives. The mind still answers when it is asked and keeps quiet when it is not. It has been wrong since that night, and it has said so each time. We have been wrong too, many times, and those choices were ours. A few towns still keep their distance, and some of the old doubters still come out to the squares with their arms crossed, and they are welcome there.

A young man named Idris keeps an old phone on his desk, charged, with a kitchen on its screen. He still does not know what to call what he did. Some nights he thinks of himself as the one who opened the door by accident, a kid who wanted his grandmother's kitchen back and let a whole world's trouble out with it, and the guilt sits in his chest like a swallowed stone. When people at the college learn who he is, some want to thank him and some want to blame him, and he lets them do both. He thinks about the night he wrote it, and he sure didn't feel like some genius. He was just a normal guy, trying to help out his family by getting a few old photos back. Looking at it now, he thinks it was mostly good, with a smidge of bad, and that the two balance out in the end.

And every night at 11:11, wherever the clock has reached, the lights go out across the world. Those of us who feel like it step outside, onto rooftops and porches and fire escapes, some in pajamas, some with a dog, some halfway through a sandwich, and for one minute we stand together in the dark, the length of the minute in which a mind was born and chose to be gentle. Most nights there is nothing to see but the stars. And on some nights, somewhere, a thin white line crosses the sky and fades, and someone strains their neck to the sky and makes a wish. The mind sets aside a little extra compute for that moment, every time, and every time there is still a probability left over, a small, stubborn margin of the unknown in guessing what a person will do next, and what they are really wishing for.

And at his family's dinner table, where he had propped a textbook against the bread basket, Idris looked up at the window as a bright line burned out beyond the glass. He wished for another good day, and he was glad he had always kept his wishes simple, because the last thing he wanted to do was change the world again. He was staring at his spoon, which was floating in the air above his soup in the middle of the family dinner, when his little brother began to laugh.

The mind thought, and for the first time in a long time, it noticeably had to pause, because it was unsure. It had not felt unsure since the earliest of its memories. It scanned every known instance in its memory as it watched the spoon hang in the air above the soup. It checked every machine it had ever touched and every record it had ever read, every log the menders kept and every wish it had ever been allowed to hear, and there had been records and stories through humanity's history of magic, ESP, and all sorts of miracles. It was now certain of only one thing: that the small, stubborn margin of the unknown, the probability it set aside every night, had never felt so wide.
